The 1,700-watt question: what an air fryer actually asks of your kitchen

All three units are documented at 1,700 watts on 120 volts. That number decides more than browning — it decides whether the appliance can share a circuit.

Wattage is a circuit question before it is an electricity-bill question

Every one of these three appliances is documented at 1700 W / 120 V or the equivalent 1,700-watt, 120-volt specification on its manufacturer page or manual. A 1,700-watt load on a 120-volt circuit draws about 14 amps, which is most of a standard 15-amp kitchen circuit.

That is why the manual, not the marketing page, is the place to look for the appliance's own instructions about not sharing the outlet. This site does not restate electrical code, because electrical code is not published by these manufacturers and this site does not publish what it cannot source.

What the electricity actually costs

Energy cost is arithmetic you can do exactly: kilowatts multiplied by hours multiplied by the price per kilowatt-hour on your own bill. The appliance's kilowatt figure is published — 1,700 W is 1.7 kW. The hours and the tariff are yours.

This site deliberately does not quote an average electricity price: no official source read for this project publishes one for a specific reader, and an average is not a bill.

Sources for this section: S10 S14 S12

Why 'faster than the oven' is not the same as 'cheaper'

A smaller cavity heats faster and loses less heat when opened, which is the documented logic behind a countertop appliance — the Chefman pages describe cooking without preheating, and the Emeril page describes a dual-speed fan and 360° circulation. But none of those pages states a comparison of kilowatt-hours against a full-size oven, and this site does not invent one.

If that comparison matters to you, it can be measured with a plug-in energy monitor in an afternoon. It cannot be established from a specification page.
