Reading the claim

“98% less oil” is a sentence on a box, not a measurement

Chefman's 8 Qt page carries the phrase in its first line. Less than what, on which food, measured how, and by whom? The page does not say. Here is exactly what it does say, and four questions that work on any percentage printed on an appliance.

What the brand page actually states

The 8 Qt TurboFry Touch page introduces the appliance with, in its own words: “extra-crispy meals with 98% less oil; perfect for veggies, pizza, frozen foods, and more”. The same sentence is in the store's product record for that page, which is where this site read it on 2026-11-17.

That is the whole claim. The page does not name a comparison — deep frying in a fryer, a recipe with more oil, another appliance, or nothing added at all. It does not name a food, a method, a lab or a date. It is one clause of marketing copy, and it is the only place in the brand's documents for this model where that number appears.

This site records it as what it is, published on the product page under a label that says so: Brand claim: meals with '98% less oil'. The label is not a hedge added by this site; it is the difference between a measurement and a promise.

Why the missing half is the important half

A percentage is a ratio, and a ratio with no denominator is not information. “98% less oil” against food submerged in a deep fryer is a real and unsurprising difference: an air fryer circulates hot air and needs a film of oil at most. “98% less oil” against the same recipe made in an oven with a brush of oil is a very different statement, and nothing on that page rules either reading in or out.

There is a second silence worth noticing. The claim sits next to a temperature range — 200–400 °F on that page — and a care rule, not next to a nutrition statement. The brand is not making a health claim here in the regulated sense; it is describing a cooking method's usual effect. Anyone who turns it into dietary advice is going beyond the document.

If you need the health question answered, it is not answerable from a spec sheet. A registered dietitian, or the published comparison the brand cites if it cites one, is the route. This site publishes neither and recommends neither.

What the manuals do say about oil — a care rule, not a diet rule

The one oil instruction in this product set is a warning about a spray can, and it is about the coating rather than the food. The dual-window manual cautions never to spray the baskets or racks with cooking oil from an aerosol can, because the harsh components can chip the nonstick; its substitute is a non-aerosol mister. The dataset row for that model reads: Nonstick, dishwasher-safe baskets. Manual caution: never spray with aerosol cooking oil — use a non-aerosol mister instead.

For the 8 Qt, this site's record carries a shorter care row: Nonstick basket and rack; top-rack dishwasher safe. No aerosol caution for that model appears in the documents read for this site, and this article does not extend the dual-window manual's rule to it. If you own the 8 Qt, the manual in the box is the authority.

The practical overlap is small and honest: the appliances in this set document how to oil them without ruining the coating. None of them documents how much oil you need for a given food, and none publishes a nutrition outcome.

Four questions that work on any printed percentage

Compared with what? If the page does not name the baseline, the number cannot be checked. A comparison to deep frying and a comparison to “no oil added” can both be true and mean opposite things.

On which food, and how much of it? A figure from a lab run on 500 g of fries is a weak guide to 200 g of vegetables. Watch for percentages that appear in marketing copy and never in the instructions.

Whose measurement is it? Brand-run, third-party, or unattributed. Only the first is common on a product page, and it is the one with an interest in the answer.

Does the manual repeat it? Instructions are the document a manufacturer has to be able to stand behind when something goes wrong. A claim that appears only in the sales copy carries a different weight, and — as with the presets and the temperature rows on this very site — the sales copy is where the published contradictions tend to live.
